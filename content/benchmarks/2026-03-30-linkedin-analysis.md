## Pattern Analysis

### Hook Patterns

1. **Contrarian take** — The dominant hook across top performers. Ethan Mollick's most-liked posts all challenge a widely held belief ("AI won't take your job", "AI detectors are fine", "IT should own AI"). Allie Miller's top recent AI post does the same ("the gap isn't about coding"). Contrarian hooks work because they create a binary: the reader either nods vigorously or wants to argue back. Both drive engagement.

2. **Provocative metaphor / naming** — Linas Beliunas' top posts consistently use a single loaded comparison as the hook: "Google just did to commerce what HTTP did to the web." This pattern frames a mundane product announcement as a civilizational event. The metaphor does interpretive work the reader carries into the rest of the post.

3. **News-jack with "death/birth" framing** — "100s of AI startups died today," "The age of KYC video verification is officially over," "RIP investment bankers?" High-urgency news-jack hooks that declare a definitive verdict rather than asking a question. These perform especially well when the underlying event is real but under-reported.

4. **Story opening** — Used by Matt Shumer (COVID parallel) and Andrew Ng (daughter's birthday cake) for their longest-form posts. Story hooks trade immediacy for depth — the reader commits to reading further because they're invested in the narrative before the argument begins.

5. **Stat + authority stacking** — Ethan Mollick's research posts: "Very big finding: randomized, controlled World Bank study." Andrew Ng's announcement posts: "From 135sec median to 8sec." Two or three credibility signals in the first sentence.

### Format Distribution

- **Text-only:** ~50% of top performers
- **Image post (with text):** ~40% of top performers (dominant for Linas Beliunas and Satya Nadella)
- **Long-form Pulse article:** ~5% (but capable of the highest raw engagement when it breaks)
- **Carousel/document:** 0% in this dataset (bestlinkedinposts.com logged 0 carousel posts for all five indexed profiles)
- **Video:** ~5% (present in profiles but none in the top-engagement tier)

**Key insight:** Text-only and image posts split the top tier. Carousels are absent from the highest-engagement posts for these influencers, though they may perform well for different audiences.

### Topic Themes

1. **AI's impact on jobs and white-collar work** — The single highest-engagement topic cluster. Matt Shumer's Pulse article, Ethan Mollick's "won't take your job" post, Allie Miller's "skills gap" post, and multiple Linas posts on bankers and developers. This theme generates debate because it is personally threatening and personally relevant to LinkedIn's audience.

2. **AI infrastructure announcements reframed as industry events** — Andrew Ng's Agentic Document Extraction (23K likes), Linas on Google UCP, Linas on Apple/Gemini. Product announcements that are re-narrated through a "what this means for the industry" lens consistently outperform raw product posts.

3. **The pace of AI progress / "you haven't tried the new models"** — Ethan Mollick's posts on AI not hitting a wall, Matt Shumer's METR timeline data, Linas on model capabilities. This theme resonates with practitioners who feel the gap between public perception and their daily experience.

4. **AI agent frameworks and agentic workflows** — Andrew Ng on Context Hub / coding agent Stack Overflow, Linas on Claude Cowork. Practitioners building with agents are a high-engagement subaudience who share technical content broadly.

5. **AI and enterprise/CEO strategy** — Satya Nadella's earnings posts, Ethan Mollick on IT not owning AI strategy, Allie Miller on systems thinking. Senior decision-makers engage with posts that validate or challenge their internal positioning.

### Structural Patterns

- **Arrow-bullet lists (→)** dominate Linas Beliunas' posts and appear in several Andrew Ng posts. These replace traditional dashes or numbers with a visual forward-motion signal. Each bullet feels like a new discovery rather than a list item.

- **Binary contrast closing** — Top posts frequently end with a sharp two-part contrast: "Seeing ≠ believing / Liveness ≠ human" (Linas), "The old edge was stamina. The new edge is orchestration" (Linas), "Not in ten years. The people building these systems say one to five years." (Matt Shumer). This gives the reader a quotable takeaway.

- **Numbered reframe** — Ethan Mollick's highest-engagement posts use numbered lists not to inform but to *reframe*: "1) Your job is more likely to transform... 2) Some jobs will really disappear..." The numbers signal structure while the content subverts the premise.

- **Single-sentence paragraphs** — Both Linas Beliunas and Matt Shumer use aggressive paragraph breaks. Sentences of 5-8 words that stand alone. This creates momentum and ensures the post reads fast on mobile.

- **P.S. substack/newsletter CTA** — Linas Beliunas ends every post with a P.S. plug for his newsletter. This functions as a brand anchor without disrupting the body of the post.

### Length and Engagement Correlation

- **Short posts (under 100 words) dominate likes** when the hook is strong. Ethan Mollick's highest-liked post is the "won't take your job" reframe at ~120 words. His moral verdict on AI detectors is ~40 words.

- **Medium posts (100-300 words) dominate comment counts** — the deliberate reframe of a complex issue generates discussion. Ethan Mollick's "AI-driven role conflict" post (200 words, 253 comments) illustrates this.

- **Long posts (300+ words) dominate shares** — Linas Beliunas and Matt Shumer's long-form posts have disproportionate share ratios. Readers share long posts because they feel like they are passing along a complete argument, not just a hot take.

- **Practical takeaway: target 150-250 words** for maximum balanced engagement (likes + comments + shares). Under 100 words favors likes. Over 300 words favors shares but sacrifices comments.

## Actionable Takeaways

1. **Open with a contrarian claim about AI and work, not an observation.** The highest-performing posts across all influencers do not say "AI is changing work." They say "The conventional wisdom about AI and work is wrong, and here is why." Name the wrong belief explicitly in the first sentence, then dismantle it. Example frame: "The [most common AI claim] is missing the real point."

2. **Use the "what this actually means" structure for all news-jack posts.** Andrew Ng and Linas Beliunas never simply report a product launch. They translate it: "What [X announcement] actually means for [Y audience]." The translation is the value. The announcement is the hook.

3. **Name emerging patterns before others do.** Ethan Mollick's "AI-driven role conflict" post generated the highest comment rate in his catalog by labeling a phenomenon practitioners were experiencing but hadn't seen articulated. For Grey AI posts: identify a behavior, tension, or shift in AI adoption that your audience is living through but hasn't named, then give it a name and define it.

4. **Use single-sentence paragraphs and arrow lists for readability on mobile.** Linas Beliunas' posts are structurally designed for a phone screen: short paragraphs, → bullets, white space between ideas. Posts that look dense on desktop scroll better on mobile. This is the format responsible for his 200+ comment counts on long posts.

5. **End with a binary contrast or verdict, not a question.** The posts that generate the most shares close with a sharp two-part statement: old world vs new world, conventional wisdom vs truth, what it was vs what it is now. Reserve the CTA question for the body of the post. The final line should feel like a punchline or headline — the sentence a reader would quote when sharing.
