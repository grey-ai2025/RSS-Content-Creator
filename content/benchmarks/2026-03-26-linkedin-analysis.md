## Pattern Analysis

### Hook Patterns

- **Contrarian take (most dominant):** Five of the fifteen top posts open by directly contradicting a belief the target audience already holds. Examples: "Prompting better might not be the skill that matters most," "You don't need a co-founder anymore," "Spectacle vs. substance" (Nadella). The formula is: name the dominant belief, then immediately dismantle it in sentence two. This forces the reader to keep reading to find out why they're wrong.

- **Stat as proof-of-concept (second most common):** Beliunas, Nadella, and Shumer all use specific verified numbers as the second move after the hook ("40,000 humans and 25,000 AI agents at McKinsey," "model overhang," "METR tasks grew from 10 minutes to 5 hours"). Stats are not decorative — they are the proof that makes the contrarian claim credible.

- **Story / personal confession (third):** Shumer's viral article and Mollick's Moltbook post both lead with first-person experience rather than analysis. "I no longer do the actual technical work of my job" is more engaging than "AI has improved dramatically." The confessional voice ("I should have told you sooner") creates vulnerability that LinkedIn audiences reward with engagement.

- **Named-era / coined phrase:** Schick's "Silicon Realism," Ng's "Turing-AGI Test," and Nadella's "model overhang" all demonstrate that creating new vocabulary is itself a high-engagement move. When you name a phenomenon, people quote you every time they use your phrase.

### Topic Themes

1. **AI capability acceleration + job displacement** — The biggest engagement driver across all profiles. Shumer's article, Nadella's "spectacle to substance" post, and multiple Beliunas posts all center on how fast AI is improving and what that means for human work. Posts that give readers explicit permission to feel worried — or hopeful — about this topic outperform neutral analysis.

2. **Geopolitics of AI / AI as power** — Nina Schick's niche but growing influence. The US-China-Europe AI race, sovereign AI infrastructure, and trade war connections are attracting growing engagement. This topic is moving from specialist to mainstream in 2026.

3. **AI workflow redesign vs. AI tool adoption** — Andrew Ng and Allie Miller both dominate this space. The distinction between "using AI" and "redesigning your workflows around AI" is a high-engagement frame because it implies most readers are doing it wrong.

4. **Specific AI tools and tactical how-tos** — Beliunas's lists of AI tools (Replit, Perplexity, Gemini), Miller's contextual vaults, and Shumer's CBS segment all perform well when they include names and specifics. Vague AI advice underperforms versus posts with named tools and concrete workflows.

5. **Named concepts / new vocabulary** — Posts that introduce a new phrase ("model overhang," "Silicon Realism," "contextual vaults," "Turing-AGI Test") are being widely cited and reshared. The phrase functions as a citation mechanism: every time someone uses the term, they implicitly credit the originator.

### Structural Patterns

- **Framework with named components** is the most common high-engagement structure. Nadella's Truth/Belief pairs, Ng's bottom-up vs. top-down framework, Schick's 5-part geopolitical model. Frameworks give readers a repeatable mental model they can share. Posts without structure — just opinions — underperform.

- **Escalating stakes narrative:** Shumer's article builds from personal anecdote → industry observation → societal consequence → action plan. This escalating-stakes arc keeps readers engaged for long-form content that would otherwise lose them.

- **Short provocative claim + evidence list:** Beliunas's most consistent pattern. One punchy opening claim, then 3–5 specific evidence points (tool names, company names, dollar figures). The claim gets engagement; the list gets saves.

### Length and Engagement Correlation

- **Long-form articles (Pulse) generate the highest absolute engagement numbers** when the topic is high-stakes and the writer has an established audience. Shumer's 5,000-word article hit 11,143 likes. But this is an outlier — most Pulse articles on these profiles get 30–50 likes.

- **Medium-length text posts (100–300 words) are the most consistent performers** across all 8 profiles. They are long enough to include a framework or evidence list but short enough to read in under 90 seconds. This is the format to default to.

- **Short posts (<100 words) work only as news-jacks or named-era declarations** — when the punch of a single phrase or stat is enough. Schick's "intelligence was scarce" opening works at ~100 words because the idea itself is dense. Random short posts do not perform.

## Actionable Takeaways

1. **Lead with the contrarian dismantling, not the insight itself.** The highest-performing hook structure is: "Everyone believes X. That is no longer true." Name the widely-held belief in sentence one, then break it in sentence two. Never bury the contrarian angle in paragraph three.

2. **Name the concept you are introducing.** If a post introduces a new way of thinking about something, give it a name in the hook. "Model overhang," "contextual vaults," "Silicon Realism" — these phrases survive beyond the post and drive return attribution. For Grey AI posts: name the behavior, the pattern, or the shift you're describing instead of describing it in a sentence.

3. **Ground every claim in at least one specific data point or company name.** Vague AI takes ("AI is changing everything") underperform posts with specifics ("McKinsey now counts 25,000 AI agents as part of its workforce"). For Grey AI posts: one real number, one named company, one named model. The specificity is what gets shared.

4. **Use the framework structure with 3–5 named components for medium-length posts.** The strongest structural pattern across all 8 influencers is: hook → framework name → 3–5 labeled components → implication. Each component should be a complete, standalone idea (not just a bullet). This is scannable, saveable, and shareable.

5. **Time news-jack posts to publish within 24–48 hours of the triggering event.** Schick's Liberation Day post, Shumer's CBS Mornings post, and Beliunas's McKinsey post all landed within the news cycle. Waiting more than 48 hours collapses the algorithmic timing advantage. For Grey AI: monitor daily for an AI story that creates a "before/after" moment and post that day.
