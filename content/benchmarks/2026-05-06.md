## Pattern Analysis

### Hook Patterns

1. **Threshold declaration in present tense (~33% of top performers):** The highest-frequency hook pattern in May 6 dataset. "Agent Mode is here in Outlook." "The AI cost war is over." "We've entered the era of autonomous teammates." "Liberation Day: The AI Trade Wars Are Here." All declare an irreversible line crossed in present tense, without hedging. The mechanism: the reader must locate themselves relative to a threshold that has already moved. The strongest version names the threshold and its consequence in the same sentence. Future-tense predictions are outcompeted by present-tense declarations across every account in this dataset. The most effective threshold posts name the displacement (what is now different) not just the capability (what AI can do).

2. **Dollar or named-metric anchor in position one (~27% of top performers):** "$37.7 billion." "4,200 likes / 491 replies." "$32 billion Ramp." "99% of users." "78% of knowledge workers." A specific number before the 50-character mark stops the feed scroll by signaling audited intelligence. The number must be counterintuitive relative to the reader's expectation — the "wait, what?" pause that earns the "See more" click. Numbers anchored to a named company outperform standalone statistics because they convert a claim into a verifiable fact. The "$37.7B equity profit" number in Beliunas's Alphabet post is the strongest example this period: the number directly disproves a two-year narrative that every LinkedIn reader held.

3. **Contrarian corporate admission hook (~20% of top performers):** "Google just admitted Claude beats Gemini at its own game." "I'm disappointed the Big Beautiful Bill didn't include a moratorium." "I built one of the first consumer AI products. Now I'm stepping back." The admission hook generates higher comment rates than the announcement hook because it signals that the named party had a prior position that they are now correcting. This creates a trust surplus for the commenter (they named the correction) and a trust deficit for the party being corrected (Google, Congress, the user who hasn't yet migrated). The Grey AI adaptation: name what a vendor admitted in their own earnings call that they wouldn't put in a press release.

4. **Insider field-report framing (~13% of top performers — Miller/Shumer pattern):** "Here's the real scoop for business professionals." "I've been using GPT-5.5 for weeks." "I work primarily out of Claude Code — it has access to my filesystem." The field-report frame converts a product claim into observed evidence. It outperforms product-review framing because the reader trusts the practitioner's experience over the analyst's opinion. Miller's Claude Code coverage in Inc. and Fast Company gives her LinkedIn posts earned credibility that compounds — the reader encounters the press coverage first, then the LinkedIn post confirms it from the practitioner directly. This is the companion-post model applied to earned media, not just self-published long-form.

5. **Reframe + named wrongness (~13% of top performers — Mollick/Schick/Ng pattern):** "Executives are asking about lab rankings — based on X rumors filtered through LinkedIn." "Liberation Day is not a trade dispute. It's the first AI war." "The Big Beautiful Bill didn't fix the right problem." All name the error the reader or audience is making before offering the correction. The named wrongness hook works at expert level — it requires the reader to already hold the wrong view being named. It generates the highest comment-per-post ratio from people who know the field well, because it challenges a working model they have actually operated with.

### Structural Patterns

- **The reframe sandwich is universal across all top 15 posts:** Open with the reframe or verdict (hook) → supply evidence with named specifics (body, 2-4 short paragraphs) → close with an implication that puts the reader inside the scenario or a question that has no predictable answer. Every top performer this period follows this structure. The CTA must not be forced binary — it must invite the reader to reveal their own position or name their own experience. "What's the first workflow your Copilot is running without you in the room?" outperforms "Do you think AI will replace your role?" The first question has an unpredictable answer from every reader; the second generates polarized votes.

- **Named specifics are non-negotiable:** "Outlook Copilot Agent Mode." "$37.7 billion equity profit." "Company Brain." "Google Cloud +63% YoY." "Josh Bickett, TypeAhead." "Claude Cowork." "78% of knowledge workers." Every top performer this period contains at least two named specifics: a named company or product, and a named number. "A major cloud company released new pricing" underperforms "Google just declared the AI cost war over" by a wide margin. Named specifics give readers language to use when sharing — reshare language is built from the post's most specific claim.

- **Single-word urgency signals (Beliunas pattern, still active):** "Wild:" — Beliunas continues to deploy single-word urgency openers on his highest-performing posts. The signal works only when the claim that follows is genuinely counterintuitive. "Wild: Google admitted Claude beats Gemini" works. "Wild: new AI feature launched" does not. The signal primes the reader that what follows requires recalibrating their working model.

- **Short declarative paragraph visual structure (universal prerequisite):** Every top performer uses 1-3 sentence paragraphs. No body text block exceeding 4 lines in the feed-visible portion. Visual scannability is a prerequisite for any hook to land in the collapsed state. The Outlook Agent Mode post (4.2K likes) is the most extreme example: sub-80 words with one sentence per idea. The algorithm measures post dwell time only after the expansion click, which requires winning the visual scan first.

- **Companion post model — highest-reach format strategy:** Mollick (Substack + companion), Beliunas (Substack note + companion), Schick (Industrial Intelligence + LinkedIn article), Nadella (sn scratchpad blog + companion). The companion post must be fully self-contained — it cannot depend on the reader clicking through. The long-form essay captures 5-10% of the companion post's audience as committed readers; the companion post gets 10-20x the reach of the essay alone.

### Length and Engagement Correlation
- **Short posts (<100 words):** Highest single-post engagement events in the dataset. The Outlook Agent Mode post (4.2K likes, 491 replies) is sub-80 words. Short posts require a news anchor or product launch to supply the motivation the post doesn't build. Short posts fail without a strong external anchor — the Copilot Cowork post (305 comments) works because the product is the anchor. The Mollick observation (<100 words) works because Wharton credibility is the anchor. Neither would work as a standalone opinion post.
- **Short-medium posts (100-250 words):** Highest-volume format. Enough structure to build a binary contrast argument; short enough to read in a feed scroll. Beliunas's financial posts, Miller's field reports, Shumer's model reviews, and Ng's policy posts all cluster here. The reframe sandwich fits precisely in this length.
- **Medium-long posts (250+ words) with named framework:** Schick's Liberation Day article and Ng's regulation framework use multiple named thesis components to sustain read-through. Framework structure is required for this length — narrative alone collapses above 300 words without a structural device.
- **Average length of top performers this period:** Short to short-medium (estimated 80-200 words body text). Hooks under 12 words: confirmed on 11 of 15 top-performing posts. All critical information lands before the 210-character feed truncation point.
