## Pattern Analysis

### Hook Patterns

- **Contrarian take (most common):** "Most people are doing this wrong," "You don't become an AI superpower by writing strategy documents," "I don't think it is good for anybody." These openers force immediate self-assessment and generate both agreement and defensive replies. Used across Shumer, Miller, Schick, Beliunas, Mollick. The key mechanic: the reader is implicitly on the wrong side of the statement until they decide otherwise.

- **Provocative claim / stat (second most common):** Quoting an extreme statement from a credible source ("AGI has been solved"), making a bold declarative prediction ("Voice AI is going to explode"), naming a specific number or trajectory. Used by Mollick, Ng, Miller, Nadella. Works because it gives readers something specific to agree with or debunk — both paths lead to engagement.

- **Personal revelation / "this happened to me first" (third):** Shumer's COVID-era comparison is the most developed example. The structure is: insider experience confirms what outsiders haven't felt yet. Also appears in Miller's voice AI post ("I go on walks where I dictate..."). First-person behavior change is more believable than abstract forecasting.

### Format Distribution
- Text-only: ~60%
- Long-form article (Pulse): ~20%
- Text with image/video: ~13%
- Carousel: ~7% (Beliunas uses occasionally; not strongly represented in top performers from this scrape)

### Topic Themes

1. **AI displacing knowledge work / jobs** — The single highest-engagement theme across all profiles. Shumer's viral piece, Beliunas's white-collar automation post, Mollick's AGI claim, Ng's Turing-AGI test, and Altman's enterprise AI statement all touch this nerve. The common thread is: AI is now affecting your job specifically, not just jobs in general.

2. **How to actually use AI better (practical workflow)** — Miller's context vault, Shumer's orchestrator setup, Ng's workflow redesign framing. The practitioner audience (2M+ LinkedIn AI followers) actively shares concrete usage frameworks because they signal expertise. Posts that include a copy-pasteable prompt, a named system ("context vault," "orchestrator thread"), or a specific workflow step dramatically increase save rates.

3. **Geopolitics and AI power / sovereignty** — Schick's "Silicon Realism" territory. Niche but high signal-to-noise ratio. Appeals to senior decision-makers and policy-adjacent audiences who do not engage with "10 ChatGPT tips" content. Posts framing AI as a matter of national infrastructure and power dynamics outperform general technology commentary with this demographic.

4. **AI terminology and trend debates (vibe coding, AGI, agentic AI)** — Beliunas on vibe coding, Mollick on AGI. Works because the audience has two camps and each post gives each camp something to share. The author does not need to be definitively on one side — framing the debate clearly is sufficient.

5. **Founder/CEO vision and prediction posts** — Nadella's "models to systems" framework, Ng's Turing-AGI Test, Altman's enterprise AI statement. These work primarily through authority and redistribution: practitioners share C-suite takes to build their own credibility. The posts succeed not because of engagement mechanics but because of source power.

### Structural Patterns

- **The named framework:** Mollick's "Turing-AGI Test," Nadella's "model overhang," Schick's "Silicon Realism," Miller's "context vault." Naming a concept is the highest-ROI move in LinkedIn thought leadership. A named framework is cited, shared with attribution, and debated — all of which extend post reach after the initial feed window closes. Every top post that introduced a named concept outperformed posts that described the same idea without naming it.

- **Three-pillar structure for executive posts:** Nadella uses numbered pillars. This structure signals depth without requiring depth — readers can scan to the third point and feel they've consumed the argument. Works particularly well for audience members who share to signal they read it rather than because they deeply engaged.

- **Short contrarian opener + principle closer:** Beliunas's vibe coding post, Shumer's coding orchestrator article. The opener is the engagement hook; the closing principle ("the real AI multiplier is knowing your craft") is the share-worthy takeaway that gets quoted in reactions and re-shares.

### Length and Engagement Correlation

- The highest absolute engagement (11,123 likes) came from Shumer's 7,000-word article — but this is an extreme outlier driven by the COVID-comparison hook going viral on X/Twitter before LinkedIn. Long-form that goes viral elsewhere and then migrates to LinkedIn performs differently than LinkedIn-native long form.
- Among LinkedIn-native posts, **medium-length (150-350 words)** posts dominate the top performer list. Long enough to demonstrate a framework, short enough to read fully in the feed without clicking.
- **Short posts (<100 words) work only when the hook is extreme enough to generate debate on its own.** Mollick's AGI claim post, Altman's enterprise AI statement. If the hook is merely good but not extreme, short posts fade.
- **Carousel format was not well-represented in top performers from this scrape.** This may reflect the profiles scraped (more academic/executive than practitioner-creator), not a broader platform trend.

## Actionable Takeaways

1. **Coin a term before writing the argument.** Every post in the top tier either introduced a named concept (Silicon Realism, context vault, model overhang, Turing-AGI Test) or referenced one. For Grey AI posts: before drafting, ask "what is the one-word or two-word label for the phenomenon this post is about?" That label should appear in the first three lines and become the thing readers cite when they share.

2. **Use the "most people are doing this wrong" opener for practitioner content.** When writing posts about AI workflows, prompting, or tool usage, lead with the failure pattern before presenting the solution. The contrarian opener is not inherently negative — it frames the reader as someone about to receive useful information they were missing. Example pattern: "[Common behavior]. And it's costing them [outcome]." Then: "The better approach is..."

3. **Anchor abstract claims in specific, first-person behavior.** Miller's "I go on walks where I dictate" and Shumer's "I tell the AI what I want, walk away for four hours, and come back to find the work done" dramatically outperform posts that describe the same capability in the third person. Grey AI posts should ground each capability claim in a first-person usage scenario, even if brief.

4. **For predictions and trend posts, take a position and make it accountable.** Miller's predictions post outperforms neutral roundups because she stakes her name to specific claims. "AI X will happen in 2026" generates more engagement than "AI X could become important." The risk of being wrong is the engagement mechanism — readers come back to agree, disagree, or update.

5. **The jobs and displacement topic remains the highest-engagement category but requires a differentiated angle.** The "AI will take your job" frame is saturated. What breaks through in 2026 is specificity: a named job category, a named timeframe with evidence, or a named mechanism ("AI is now writing 'much of the code' at Anthropic, per Dario Amodei"). Grey AI posts should anchor displacement claims to specific data points (METR task-length doubling, Amodei's 50% entry-level estimate) rather than vague futures, and should include either an action the reader can take or a position the reader can adopt.
