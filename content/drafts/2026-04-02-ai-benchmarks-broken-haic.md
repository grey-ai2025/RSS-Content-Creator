## HEADLINE
A UCL/Stanford researcher argues AI benchmarks are structurally broken — they test AI against individuals in isolation, but AI always performs inside teams and workflows where the results are entirely different.

## STRATEGY
- Audience: Enterprise CIOs, product leaders, procurement teams, HR and L&D professionals, and anyone responsible for selecting, evaluating, or justifying AI tool investments.
- Current belief: If an AI model scores 98% on a benchmark and outperforms human experts in tests, it will improve outcomes when deployed in our organization.
- Reframe: Benchmark scores measure performance in a context that does not exist in practice. AI is always deployed inside teams, workflows, and organizations — where performance is relational, time-dependent, and systemic. A model that outperforms a radiologist in a benchmark can slow down an entire hospital workflow.
- Practical consequence: Every enterprise making multi-million-dollar AI adoption decisions based on leaderboard scores is accepting undisclosed deployment risk. The benchmark was not designed to predict what happens when this model joins your team.
- Core claim: "AI is almost never used the way it is benchmarked" — and the gap between benchmark performance and real-world team performance is the most expensive blind spot in enterprise AI procurement.
- Tension point: Regulatory frameworks and vendor sales cycles are both built on benchmark scores that don't measure operational reality. The risk is structural, not incidental.
- CTA direction: Reframe-question (what evaluation criteria are you using that actually predict real-world team performance?)

## KEY FACTS
- FDA-approved AI radiology tools that outperformed radiologists in benchmarks consistently introduced delays in real hospital workflows due to misalignment with multidisciplinary reporting standards
- Current benchmarks test AI against individual humans in isolation — but AI is always deployed inside teams, workflows, and organizations where performance is relational
- HAIC benchmark framework reframes evaluation across 4 dimensions: unit shifts from individual to team; time horizon expands to longitudinal; outcome measures expand to coordination quality and error detectability; system effects replace isolated outputs
- High benchmark scores create a direct path to the "AI graveyard": adoption followed by abandonment when real-world gaps emerge, eroding organizational trust
- Author's key finding: "AI is almost never used the way it is benchmarked"
- Source credibility: Professor Angela Aristidou, University College London / Stanford HAI; research spanning healthcare, humanitarian, SME, and higher education sectors in UK, US, and Asia since 2022

## GREY AI ANGLE
Benchmark scores are the primary input for enterprise AI procurement decisions and regulatory approval processes. A framework that measures individual task performance but cannot predict team-level or workflow-level outcomes means organizations are making AI adoption decisions that expose them to deployment risk they cannot see until it is too late to recover vendor trust. AI literacy in 2026 means asking not "does this model outscore humans?" but "does this model improve what our team produces, over time, in our actual workflow?" — and most current procurement criteria don't ask that question.

## RAW MATERIAL
Professor Angela Aristidou (UCL / Stanford HAI) argues that current AI benchmarks are structurally broken: they test AI performance in isolation against individual humans, but AI is always deployed inside teams, workflows, and organizations where performance is relational and emerges over time. FDA-approved AI radiology tools that outperformed radiologists in benchmarks consistently introduced delays in real hospital workflows due to misalignment with multidisciplinary reporting standards. Research spanned healthcare, humanitarian, nonprofit, SME, and higher education organizations in UK, US, and Asia since 2022. In a UK hospital study (2021-2024), shifting from individual diagnostic accuracy to team coordination and error-detectability metrics revealed outcomes that single-task benchmarks missed entirely. The proposed HAIC framework evaluates across 4 dimensions: unit of analysis (individual → team/workflow), time horizon (one-off → longitudinal), outcome measures (accuracy → coordination quality and error detectability), and scope (isolated outputs → system effects). Key quote: "AI is almost never used the way it is benchmarked."
