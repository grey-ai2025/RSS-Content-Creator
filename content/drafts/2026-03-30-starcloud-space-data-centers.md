## HEADLINE
A startup that trained an AI model in orbit just raised $170M at a $1.1B valuation — the fastest YC company to reach unicorn status.

## STRATEGY
- Audience: Enterprise and infrastructure leaders, investors, and technology strategists tracking where AI compute is physically heading.
- Current belief: AI infrastructure is a land problem — data centers, power grids, cooling systems. The constraints are terrestrial and the solutions will be too.
- Reframe: The power and land constraints of Earth-based AI infrastructure are severe enough that institutional investors are betting orbital compute is not science fiction but a near-term alternative.
- Practical consequence: The AI infrastructure investment thesis has expanded from chips and cables to satellites — and the gap between terrestrial supply and AI demand is the engine driving it.
- Core claim: The reason a startup is building data centers in space isn't ambition — it's that the ground is running out of room.
- Tension point: The economics depend on Starship, which won't be commercially available until 2028-2029. This is a serious bet on a single dependency.
- CTA direction: Reframe-question (what does it mean that orbit is now a viable answer to terrestrial AI compute constraints?)

## KEY FACTS
- $170M Series A at a $1.1B valuation — fastest YC startup to reach unicorn status, 17 months post demo day
- First satellite launched November 2025 with an Nvidia H100; claimed first AI model trained in orbit
- Starcloud 2 will carry Nvidia Blackwell chips and an AWS server blade
- Target cost: $0.05/kWh — competitive with terrestrial data centers — but contingent on Starship commercial launch cadence (expected 2028–2029)
- The entire Starlink network (10,000 satellites) produces ~200 megawatts; US terrestrial data centers under construction exceed 25 gigawatts — a 125x gap
- Source credibility: Series A led by Benchmark and EQT Ventures; competitors include Google Project Suncatcher and Aetherflux (reportedly at $2B valuation)

## GREY AI ANGLE
AI's compute demands are outpacing what terrestrial infrastructure can physically deliver — and orbital data centers are being funded as a direct response to that constraint. For enterprise leaders planning AI infrastructure strategy, this signals that the "where does the compute live" question is no longer bounded by geography. Organizational readiness for the next layer of AI infrastructure now includes understanding dependencies that extend literally off-planet.
