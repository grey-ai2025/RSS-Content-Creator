## HEADLINE
The metric governments and AI labs use to measure AI's threat to jobs — "task exposure" — is fundamentally flawed, and an economist is calling for a Manhattan Project to collect the data that would actually predict displacement.

## STRATEGY
- Audience: Workforce strategists, HR leaders, operations executives, and any manager currently making headcount or reskilling decisions based on published AI-and-jobs research.
- Current belief: Task exposure scores — "real estate agents are 28% exposed to AI" — are meaningful indicators of which jobs and roles are at risk from AI automation. The research backing these numbers comes from credible institutions including OpenAI and Anthropic.
- Reframe: Exposure to AI tasks tells you nothing about whether demand for that work will change. The missing variable is price elasticity — how much demand shifts when AI makes a service cheaper to produce. Without it, every confident prediction about AI job displacement is built on an incomplete model. The institutions publishing these numbers know this.
- Practical consequence: Managers making headcount decisions, executives projecting future workforce needs, and workers deciding whether to reskill are all navigating on data that economists now say is categorically insufficient. The playbook for AI workforce planning has a structural hole in it.
- Core claim: Job exposure metrics measure AI's reach — not AI's impact. The data needed to predict actual displacement does not yet exist, and no institution has committed to collecting it at the scale required.
- Tension point: The same AI labs using O*NET exposure data to describe their impact on labor markets are the ones saying agentic AI "can do all jobs within five years" — and the economist calling the measurement meaningless has data on grocery scanners but not on tutors, web developers, or dietitians.
- CTA direction: Reframe-question (is your workforce strategy built on exposure metrics or elasticity data — and do you know the difference?)

## KEY FACTS
- OpenAI used the US government's O*NET task catalogue in December 2025 to calculate job exposure — e.g., real estate agents rated 28% exposed
- Anthropic used the same data to cross-reference which tasks people actually use Claude to complete
- University of Chicago economist Alex Imas: "Exposure alone is a completely meaningless tool for predicting displacement"
- Price elasticity data exists for groceries (supermarket scanner partnerships) but not for professionals — tutors, web developers, dietitians — where AI displacement matters most
- Economists at the Yale Budget Lab, previously skeptical of AI job threat, are now reconsidering their positions
- A societal impacts researcher at Anthropic has publicly warned of a "breakdown of the early-career ladder" in the near term
- Source credibility: University of Chicago economics research; referenced by one of the most credible academic institutions on labor economics; Imas's critique is on record with attributed quotes

## GREY AI ANGLE
Every organization building an AI workforce strategy is making decisions on data that leading economists now say is categorically insufficient. AI literacy in 2026 includes understanding what job exposure metrics actually measure — and what they cannot. Teams using O*NET-based exposure scores to prioritize reskilling programs or restructure roles are applying a measurement tool that was designed for a different question. The data infrastructure required to actually model displacement at role and industry level — price elasticity collected economy-wide — does not exist. That is not a minor methodological caveat. It is the entire foundation of the planning model.
