## HEADLINE
A multi-university study (Carnegie Mellon, MIT, Oxford, UCLA) found that as little as 10 minutes of AI assistance measurably reduces a person's willingness to persist through difficulty once the AI is removed — with the lead researcher naming the "gradual disempowerment" risk and warning that answer-giving AI may have "very different long-term effects" than coaching-style AI.

## STRATEGY
- Audience: Founders and team leads at 10-50 person companies who are running AI-first workflows and measuring productivity gains — but have not yet asked what those gains are trading away in terms of their team's long-term problem-solving capability.
- Current belief: AI tools make my team more productive. More productivity means better outcomes. If some tasks are now handled by AI, my team's time is freed for higher-value work. The capability question doesn't apply to my team because we're using AI as a tool, not a crutch — we know when to rely on it and when to think for ourselves.
- Reframe: The study's finding isn't about AI addiction or replacing human judgment on individual tasks. It is about the mechanism by which the willingness to persist through difficulty — the primary predictor of skill acquisition — gets quietly eroded with regular AI use. Ten minutes of AI assistance was enough to produce a measurable change in participants' follow-through when the AI was removed. For a team running AI-first workflows, this means every quarter of AI-assisted productivity may be producing an unmeasured and compounding reduction in the team's capacity to solve novel problems without AI assistance. The productivity numbers look good. The capability trajectory may not.
- Practical consequence: The deployment decision that matters most is not "which AI tool does my team use" — it is "does the AI tool we use give answers or coach thinking?" Answer-delivery tools optimize for immediate productivity at the expense of the persistence and judgment development that compounds into long-term team capability. Coaching-style tools optimize for the reverse. For any founder who plans to keep running their business after the AI landscape changes again, the capability their team is building — or not building — right now is a strategic variable they are currently not measuring.
- Core claim: AI tools optimized for immediate productivity may be creating a hidden capability debt: teams get faster at current tasks while gradually losing the willingness to persist through the difficulty that builds the judgment to handle novel ones.
- Tension point: The study's lead researcher at MIT named "gradual disempowerment" as the risk — not occasional over-reliance, but a systematic and measurable shift in the willingness to struggle that the researchers could produce in a controlled experiment in 10 minutes. If that effect compounds across hundreds of working hours per year on an AI-assisted team, the capability gap between teams that build with AI and teams that build through AI may not show up in quarterly productivity metrics. It will show up when something new and hard appears and the team that used AI to go faster realizes it no longer knows how to go slow.
- CTA direction: Reframe-question — if your team's AI tools are optimized for answer-delivery rather than coaching, what capability are you building vs. trading away over the next 12 months?

## KEY FACTS
- Three experiments, each involving several hundred participants — tasks included simple fractions and reading comprehension (foundational cognitive skills)
- When AI was removed mid-task, AI-assisted participants were "significantly more likely to give up or flub their answers" than control participants
- As little as 10 minutes of AI assistance was sufficient to produce a measurable difference in persistence
- Lead researcher: MIT Assist. Prof. Michiel Bakker — "We should be more careful about what kind of help AI provides, and when"
- Bakker's key distinction: "Systems that give direct answers may have very different long-term effects from systems that scaffold, coach, or challenge the user"
- Institutions: Carnegie Mellon, MIT, Oxford, UCLA — four named research universities, peer-reviewed methodology
- Agentic AI systems flagged as "particularly unpredictable" because they complete complex chores independently and can introduce errors in ways that further reduce the user's oversight capacity
- OpenAI has already been attempting to reduce sycophancy in newer GPT releases as a named related concern
- Source credibility: Multi-university collaboration (Carnegie Mellon, MIT, Oxford, UCLA) — peer-reviewed academic research with named lead researcher and named institutional affiliations
