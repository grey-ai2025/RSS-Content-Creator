## HEADLINE
Sullivan & Cromwell — one of the most prestigious law firms in the United States — submitted a court filing with approximately three dozen AI-generated errors, including fabricated case citations, that were caught by opposing counsel, not by the firm's own review process.

## STRATEGY
- Audience: Founders and operators at 10-50 person companies who use AI to produce documents, proposals, contracts, reports, or any output that will be read and acted upon by a client, partner, regulator, or counterparty.
- Current belief: AI hallucinations are a known risk I manage by spot-checking outputs. If I'm using a reputable tool and I've worked with it enough to know its tendencies, my review process is sufficient. This is a problem for people who don't know how to use AI tools — not a structural risk for organizations with capable AI users.
- Reframe: Sullivan & Cromwell is not a cautionary tale about naive AI use. It is a firm with billions in annual revenue, hundreds of attorneys, and a brand staked on precision. The errors were not caught by their review process — they were caught by opposing counsel. The failure mode is not carelessness. It is the specific property of AI-generated text that makes errors invisible to the person who prompted the tool: hallucinations are fluent, authoritative, and structurally indistinguishable from correct outputs. The review process failed not because it was inadequate — but because the AI's output doesn't flag its own fabrications. The professional liability is not theoretical. Lawyers have already been fined $110,000 for the same failure in an Oregon winery case just weeks earlier.
- Practical consequence: Any founder whose team uses AI to produce outputs that a third party will act on — contracts, proposals, client reports, compliance documents, due diligence memos — has the same structural exposure S&C had. The question is not whether your AI tool hallucinates (it does). The question is whether your review process is designed to catch hallucinations specifically — not just errors generally. Those are different processes.
- Core claim: The Sullivan & Cromwell filing is not an AI misuse story — it is an AI review process story. The failure was not in using AI. It was in not having a verification workflow designed specifically for the hallucination failure mode.
- Tension point: The errors were caught by the other side, not by the firm. In a client relationship, "caught by your counterpart" means the damage is already done before your review process is even triggered.
- CTA direction: Opinion-bait question — does your team have a review process designed specifically to catch AI hallucinations in outputs your clients or counterparties will act on?

## GREY AI ANGLE
The Sullivan & Cromwell story surfaces the most under-addressed AI governance gap in small business operations: the difference between a generic error-checking process and a hallucination-specific verification workflow. Most AI governance frameworks tell teams to "review AI outputs before submitting" — but hallucinations are not caught by general review because they are fluent and authoritative. AI literacy in 2026 includes knowing that verification for AI-generated factual claims (citations, statistics, attributions, source references) requires a different process than proofreading or editing. The question for any team producing AI-assisted documents is whether their review workflow is designed to catch fabrications specifically — or only to catch errors that look like errors.
