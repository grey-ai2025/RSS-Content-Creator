## HEADLINE
"AI psychosis" — the cognitive failure where CEOs extrapolate demo results into company-wide automation decisions — has produced 115,430 tech layoffs in five months while a UC Berkeley meta-analysis finds no robust relationship between AI adoption and aggregate productivity gain.

## STRATEGY
- Audience: Founders and team leads at 10-50 person companies who are actively making or about to make workforce decisions (headcount restructuring, role elimination, hiring freezes) based on AI capability assessments — including those who have seen AI demos that look more capable than their day-to-day production reality.
- Current belief: The demo represents the production capability. If AI handles this task in a demo, it can replace the person doing that task. CEOs who are cutting headcount to fund AI are making a rational trade — the productivity gains justify the decision.
- Reframe: The demo is the happy path. Production is everything else. The gap between what a CEO sees in a demo and what ships in production is where the AI psychosis lives — and the data shows it is a large gap. UC Berkeley found no robust productivity-gain relationship in AI adoption at scale. MIT researchers put full agent competence at 80-95% quality by 2029. The ClickUp CEO deployed 3,000 agents and cut 22% of staff — both at once. The bottleneck didn't disappear; Harvard found it moved to the executives who now approve all agent output.
- Practical consequence: Companies that cut people to fund agents are discovering they destroyed institutional knowledge and moved the bottleneck. The Harvard finding is the sharpest: mass AI delegation doesn't remove the human approval step — it concentrates all approvals at the executive layer, creating organizational chaos. For founders making these decisions now, the actionable audit is whether their AI deployment is above the MIT competence threshold for their specific tasks — not the vendor's benchmark tasks.
- Core claim: AI psychosis isn't a CEO character flaw — it's a structural information gap: the demo optimizes for impressiveness, production optimizes for reliability, and 115,430 layoffs in five months were made by leaders who conflated the two.
- Tension point: Box CEO Aaron Levie named and defined the condition — "CEOs are uniquely prone to AI psychosis because they're sufficiently distant from the last mile of work" — while being a CEO himself. The most credible voice on the demo-production gap is the one admitting the vulnerability from the inside.
- CTA direction: Opinion-bait — every founder who has watched an AI demo and felt the gap between what it showed and what they shipped has a strong, specific position on where the psychosis line sits.

## KEY FACTS
- 115,430 tech layoffs in the first five months of 2026 across 152 companies — nearly matching all of 2025 (124,636 across 275 companies)
- UC Berkeley California Management Review meta-analysis: "no robust relationship between AI adoption and aggregate productivity gain"
- MIT researchers: AI agents can't perform human-quality work on many tasks; competence threshold (80-95%) not projected until 2029
- Harvard Business Review: mass AI delegation moves the bottleneck to executives who must approve all agent output — creates organizational chaos rather than efficiency
- ClickUp CEO deployed ~3,000 internal AI agents and cut 22% of staff simultaneously — the defining case study
- Box CEO Aaron Levie: "CEOs are uniquely prone to AI psychosis because they're sufficiently distant from the last mile of work that still has to happen"
- Source credibility: Named research institutions (UC Berkeley, MIT, Harvard Business Review); named public company CEOs (Levie, ClickUp's Zeb Evans); named case study with specific numbers (3,000 agents, 22% staff reduction)

## GREY AI ANGLE
AI psychosis is an AI literacy failure at the leadership layer. The demo optimizes for capability on a curated task set. Production exposes failure modes on edge cases, data quality issues, integration friction, and the last-mile work that doesn't appear in demos. Founders who use AI tools themselves — for their own actual work, not for demos — develop a calibrated sense of the capability threshold that CEO-level demos systematically bypass. AI organizational readiness in 2026 includes building the mechanism for leadership to receive honest capability assessments from the people closest to the production work — not the vendor's demo environment. The Harvard bottleneck finding is the organizational consequence of skipping that mechanism: all the approvals now go to the people furthest from the last mile.
