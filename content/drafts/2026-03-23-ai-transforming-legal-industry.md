## HEADLINE
AI has moved from experimental to operational inside major law firms — compressing the time required for contract review, legal research, due diligence, and drafting — and is now actively forcing the profession to rethink billing models and associate headcount pipelines.

## KEY FACTS
- AI is now displacing associate-level billable work across contract review, legal research, due diligence, and document drafting — tasks that previously justified significant hourly rates
- Law firms are actively repricing services as AI compresses what previously took junior teams days into hours
- Corporate legal departments are simultaneously adopting AI tools to reduce outside counsel spend — squeezing law firm revenue from both sides
- Source credibility: Financial Times analysis; corroborated by named practice area use cases and structural economic framing

## GREY AI ANGLE
Law is one of the most credential-gated, relationship-driven professional domains in existence — and AI is materially restructuring its economics. That is the signal for every other knowledge-intensive profession. The pattern is consistent: AI absorbs high-volume, lower-judgment work that juniors do, compresses the expertise-building pipeline, and puts pressure on the billing models the profession was structured around. Consulting, accounting, finance, and medicine are watching the legal industry the way law firms watched what happened to journalism. The question for leaders is not whether AI is coming for their sector. It is whether they have a framework for the transition before the economics force one.

## RAW MATERIAL
The early failure mode — AI fabricating case citations that didn't exist — has been resolved through validation workflows; the profession has moved past the hallucination problem to the economics problem. The squeeze is bilateral: law firms face AI-enabled internal efficiency pressure from clients whose legal departments now use AI tools to challenge billing; and external pressure from the same AI compressing the firm's own delivery costs while reducing the juniors who previously justified high rates. The "business of law" framing is deliberate — this is not about how lawyers practice, it is about how law firms make money. The parallel to journalism is instructive: a credential-gated, specialist profession where AI first disrupted the workflow layer before restructuring the economic model.
