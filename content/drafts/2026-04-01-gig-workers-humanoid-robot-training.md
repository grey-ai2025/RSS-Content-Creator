## HEADLINE
Thousands of gig workers across 50+ countries are filming themselves doing household chores — folding laundry, washing dishes, ironing — to train humanoid robots, earning $15/hour while powering a $6 billion industry whose timeline is longer than the hype suggests.

## STRATEGY
- Audience: Technology leaders, future-of-work professionals, AI strategists, and anyone following the AI infrastructure story — particularly those who assume the humanoid robot revolution is imminent and frictionless.
- Current belief: Humanoid robot development is advancing rapidly through AI breakthroughs, with companies like Tesla and Figure AI close to deploying autonomous robots in homes and factories.
- Reframe: The gap between demo and deployment is being bridged by a new global gig economy of human workers filming chores. The "automated future" requires enormous human labor to train — and the data bottleneck means the timeline is longer than the headlines suggest.
- Practical consequence: The humanoid robot industry needs more real-world training data than LLMs required — and that data can only come from human movement. The industry is structurally dependent on a global gig labor market that raises serious questions about consent, data ownership, and timeline.
- Core claim: Every humanoid robot demo is powered by thousands of contract workers in developing economies filming themselves doing chores for $15/hour — and the industry needs orders of magnitude more of this data before robots can match what those workers are demonstrating.
- Tension point: The workers being filmed do not know which robotics companies receive their footage or how it will be shared — and the robots being trained may eventually replace the types of work those same workers depend on.
- CTA direction: Reframe-question (what does the labor infrastructure underneath AI actually look like — and does your mental model of "AI is automated" account for it?)

## KEY FACTS
- One company has hired thousands of contract workers in 50+ countries (Nigeria, India, Argentina) to film household chores with iPhones strapped to their foreheads at $15/hour
- Footage sold to humanoid robotics companies including Tesla, Figure AI, and Agility Robotics
- Robotics industry spent over $6 billion on humanoid development in 2025; companies spending $100M+/year on real-world training data
- Scale AI has gathered 100,000+ hours of movement footage; the hiring company has "tens of thousands of hours"
- A UC Berkeley roboticist: humanoid robots may need even more training data than LLMs — "it's going to take longer than people think"
- Source credibility: Named company (Micro1), named robotics clients (Tesla, Figure AI, Agility Robotics), independent academic expert (UC Berkeley), named competitor (Scale AI) with corroborated figures

## GREY AI ANGLE
The humanoid robot training pipeline is the clearest illustration of a pattern running throughout AI infrastructure: human labor at scale is the input that makes AI capability possible. For professionals tracking AI readiness and AI's economic impact, this reframes the "automation eliminates labor" narrative. The transition to physical AI is not frictionless — it requires a new global labor market, data governance infrastructure, and consent frameworks that do not yet exist. The AI timeline most organizations are planning around may be materially longer than the funding announcements suggest.
