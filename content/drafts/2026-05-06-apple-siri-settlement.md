## HEADLINE
Apple agreed to pay $250 million to settle a class-action lawsuit over Siri AI features that were advertised at launch but never shipped — marking the first time a major tech company faces direct financial consequences for AI capability overpromising.

## STRATEGY
- Audience: Founders, SMB operators, and team leads at 10-50 person companies who are currently evaluating AI vendor pitches, building products with AI features, or making purchasing decisions based on roadmap promises from AI vendors.
- Current belief: AI overpromising is a vendor credibility problem, not a legal liability. If a tool doesn't ship what was demo'd, we switch vendors. The downside is wasted time, not financial exposure. That kind of accountability only applies to enterprise contracts with specific SLA clauses — not to product marketing.
- Reframe: Apple's settlement changes the category. "Available now" language in AI product marketing is no longer just a credibility risk — it is a class-action trigger. The same claim pattern Apple used ("Apple Intelligence is available now") is the standard pitch format for AI vendors in B2B sales: roadmap features presented as current capabilities. For any founder who has signed a vendor contract based on a feature demo of something not yet in production, or who is marketing AI capabilities their own product has not yet shipped, the Apple precedent is a legal risk model, not just a cautionary tale.
- Practical consequence: Any operator evaluating AI vendors needs to now treat the gap between what a vendor demos and what is in production as a potential liability question, not just a due diligence question. Any operator building products with AI features needs to audit their own marketing language for "available now" claims that do not yet match shipped functionality. The precedent is set at $250 million — and Apple admitted no fault, meaning the standard for liability does not require fraud.
- Core claim: Apple's $250M settlement establishes that AI capability overpromising is no longer a reputational risk — it is a legal one, and the "available now" language pattern that triggered it is standard across the AI vendor ecosystem.
- Tension point: Apple pulled a TV ad for a Siri feature that wasn't ready, and confirmed the enhanced Siri would take "longer than we thought" — internal admissions that the public marketing had outrun the engineering. That sequence (marketing ahead of shipping, private acknowledgment of the gap, public settlement) is a pattern any AI vendor or AI-forward startup is one lawsuit away from repeating.
- CTA direction: Opinion-bait — every founder who has sat through an AI vendor demo of features "coming soon" has an opinion about whether the Apple precedent changes how they evaluate those pitches.

## KEY FACTS
- Settlement amount: $250 million common fund — one of Apple's largest settlements ever
- Eligible claimants: US buyers of iPhone 15 or iPhone 16, June 10, 2024 through March 29, 2025
- Payout per device: $25 presumptive, up to $95 depending on claims volume
- The advertising regulator BBB's National Advertising Division found Apple's "available now" claim for Apple Intelligence to be misleading before the lawsuit settled
- Apple pulled a TV ad for Siri features that weren't ready and privately confirmed to press that enhanced Siri would take "longer than we thought"
- This is Apple's second Siri-related settlement in two years — the first ($95M) was over claims Siri recorded private conversations
- Source credibility: Named court settlement with publicly disclosed terms; BBB National Advertising Division finding on record; Apple's own public statement confirming feature delay

## CREDIBILITY LAYER (mandatory unless news-jack)

1. **Source attribution**: The $250 million settlement is a named, filed class-action resolution — publicly verifiable through court records. The BBB's National Advertising Division finding (that Apple's "available now" claim was misleading) is a named regulatory body's published determination. Apple's own statement and the pulled TV ad are documented public events, not analyst interpretations.
2. **One concrete fact-checkable detail**: Apple confirmed to Daring Fireball in March 2025 that enhanced Siri features would take "longer than we thought" and simultaneously pulled a running TV advertisement that featured the not-yet-available functionality. Both the retraction and the statement are public, timestamped, and independently verifiable — making the gap between marketing and shipping a documented, not alleged, fact.
3. **Institutional self-report stat**: Apple's own settlement terms — $25 per device, up to $95 per device depending on claim volume, covering a defined nine-month purchase window — are the company's self-reported acknowledgment of the financial scale of AI marketing liability. Apple admitted no wrongdoing, meaning the $250M is a legal cost estimate for the "available now" claim pattern, not an admission of fraud.

**Is this a news-jack?** No — the settlement requires analytical framing about what AI marketing liability means for founders evaluating vendors and building AI-forward products. The settlement is the fact; the "available now" claim pattern as a replicable legal risk is the brief's contribution.
