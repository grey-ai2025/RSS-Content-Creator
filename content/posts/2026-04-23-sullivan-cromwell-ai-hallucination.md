# Sullivan & Cromwell AI Hallucination — Review Process Design Gap

## Caption

Sullivan & Cromwell — one of the most elite law firms in the United States — submitted a court filing with approximately three dozen AI-generated errors. Opposing counsel caught them. The firm's own review process did not.

Two weeks earlier, lawyers in an Oregon winery case were fined $110,000 for the same failure: AI-generated bogus citations that passed their review.

The failure mode isn't carelessness. It's something more structural.

AI hallucinations are fluent. Authoritative-sounding. They pass the same reading a careful proofreader applies — because the fabricated citation looks identical to a real one until someone checks the source. A general review catches mistakes that look like mistakes. Hallucinations don't look like mistakes.

Any team producing AI-assisted documents — proposals, contracts, client reports — has the same exposure. The question isn't whether your AI tool hallucinates. It's whether your review process was designed to catch hallucinations, or only errors that already look wrong.

Does your team have a hallucination-specific review process for AI-generated documents — or a general review that would have passed the Sullivan & Cromwell filing the same way their process did?

#AIGovernance #EnterpriseAI

## Strategy Brief

- Audience: Founders and operators at 10-50 person companies who use AI to produce documents, proposals, contracts, reports, or any client-facing output that a third party will act on
- Reframe: The S&C story is not about naive AI use — it is about the structural difference between a general document review (catches errors that look like errors) and a hallucination-specific verification workflow (catches fluent, authoritative-sounding fabrications). The failure was not in using AI. It was in not having the right review process.
- Consequence: Any founder whose team uses AI to produce client-facing outputs has the same structural exposure S&C had. The review process most teams run is designed for human errors — not for the specific failure mode AI introduces.
- Core claim: Proofreading is not a hallucination filter. Every team producing AI-assisted documents needs to understand the difference — before opposing counsel, a client, or a regulator explains it for them.

Rewrite: compress into "Two law firms. Two review processes. Neither caught the fabrications." Tighter, loses the repetition tell.

In this case the three items are genuinely different and the reader needs all three to recognize their own situation. Kept but moved to a standalone sentence to break the density.

Same meaning, 6 fewer words, punchier.

**Rewrites applied:**

- Triple parallelism ("Two law firms. Two sets of lawyers...") compressed: "Two law firms. Two review processes. Neither caught the fabrications."

- "Most don't know it yet. Sullivan & Cromwell learned from opposing counsel." — retained as-is. This two-sentence close is the Kurt Castro voice at its sharpest: dry, precise, slightly cutting. The name lands as the payoff.
