## Summary
The standard measure used to assess AI's threat to jobs — "task exposure" — is fundamentally flawed, according to University of Chicago economist Alex Imas. While research tools like the US government's O*NET task catalogue have been used by OpenAI and Anthropic to calculate how "exposed" jobs are to AI automation, Imas argues that exposure alone tells us almost nothing about actual displacement. The missing variable is price elasticity: how much demand for a product or service changes when AI makes it cheaper to produce. Without this data collected economy-wide, policymakers and workers are navigating blind. Imas is calling for what he describes as a "Manhattan Project" to collect this data across the entire economy — a project that does not yet exist.

## Key Data Points
- OpenAI used the O*NET task catalogue in December 2025 to calculate job exposure to AI — e.g., real estate agents rated 28% exposed
- Anthropic used the same data to cross-reference which tasks people actually use Claude to complete
- Economists who previously dismissed AI job threats are now reconsidering — Yale Budget Lab among those shifting position
- A societal impacts researcher at Anthropic publicly warned of a "breakdown of the early-career ladder" in the near term
- Dario Amodei has described AI as "a general labor substitute for humans" that could do all jobs within five years
- Price elasticity data exists for grocery items (University of Chicago supermarket scanner partnerships) but not for professionals like tutors, web developers, or dietitians
- Imas: "Exposure alone is a completely meaningless tool for predicting displacement"
- Agentic AI and reasoning models "can rack up quite a bill" — meaning AI replacement is not always cheaper than the human it would replace

## Why It Matters
Every professional following the AI-and-jobs debate is reacting to numbers that economists now say are fundamentally misleading. The "exposure" framework sounds precise but actually tells you very little about whether your role will shrink, grow, or transform. The real determinant — how much demand for your work changes when AI makes it cheaper to produce — has never been systematically measured. This matters to every manager making headcount decisions, every IC wondering whether to reskill, and every executive trying to project future workforce needs. Until the right data exists, confident predictions about AI job displacement are built on sand.
