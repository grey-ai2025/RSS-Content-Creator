# The Global Gig Workers Filming Their Chores to Train Humanoid Robots

**Title:** The gig workers who are training humanoid robots at home

**Source URL:** https://www.technologyreview.com/2026/04/01/1134863/humanoid-data-training-gig-economy-2026-breakthrough-technology/

**Publication Date:** April 1, 2026

**Keyword Match:** generative AI moving from hype to pragmatism; AI in HR and workforce; agentic AI infrastructure (physical AI / humanoid robot training pipeline)

## Summary

A global gig economy has emerged to solve one of the most critical bottlenecks in humanoid robotics: real-world movement data. Companies like Micro1 have hired thousands of contract workers across more than 50 countries — Nigeria, India, Argentina — to strap iPhones to their foreheads and film themselves doing household chores: folding laundry, washing dishes, ironing. The footage is sold to robotics companies including Tesla, Figure AI, and Agility Robotics to train their humanoid robots. The work pays $15 an hour and is providing meaningful income in lower-wage economies, but raises serious questions about data privacy, informed consent, and whether gig workers understand how their footage will be used. As MIT Technology Review designated humanoid robots a 2026 Breakthrough Technology, this story reveals the unglamorous but essential labor infrastructure underneath the AI revolution.

## Key Data Points

- Micro1 (Palo Alto) has hired thousands of contract workers in 50+ countries to record household chore footage
- Workers are paid approximately $15/hour — competitive income in markets like Nigeria and India with high unemployment
- Footage sold to humanoid robotics companies including Tesla, Figure AI, and Agility Robotics
- Robotics industry spent over $6 billion on humanoid robot development in 2025
- Companies spending more than $100 million per year on real-world training data (Micro1 CEO estimate)
- Scale AI has gathered more than 100,000 hours of movement footage; Micro1 has "tens of thousands of hours"
- DoorDash now pays delivery drivers to film themselves doing chores — expanding the data economy
- In China, state-run robot training centers employ workers wearing VR headsets and exoskeletons
- Workers vetted and managed by an AI agent named "Zara" that conducts interviews and reviews video samples
- UC Berkeley roboticist Ken Goldberg: humanoid robots may need even more training data than LLMs — "it's going to take longer than people think"
- Workers are not told which robotics companies receive their footage or how it will be shared

## Why It Matters

This story exposes the human labor infrastructure underneath the "AI is automated" narrative. Every humanoid robot demo at a trade show is powered by thousands of contract workers in developing economies filming themselves folding laundry for $15 an hour. For professionals thinking about AI strategy and AI's economic impact, this is a grounding story: the transition to physical AI is not frictionless automation — it requires a new global labor market that raises real questions about data ownership, consent, and economic equity. It also reframes the humanoid robot timeline: the data collection bottleneck is real, the work is happening at scale, but UC Berkeley's framing ("it's going to take longer than people think") provides a useful counterweight to the hype cycle.
