## Summary

A new Gallup report based on nearly 1,600 respondents ages 14 to 29 across the United States documents a striking divergence in how Gen Z relates to AI: usage is rising as AI embeds itself more deeply in school and work, but emotional sentiment is moving sharply negative. The generation that grew up with smartphones and was expected to be AI's most enthusiastic adopters is instead becoming its most skeptical critics. Gallup finds that young adults have grown less hopeful and more angry about artificial intelligence over the past year — even as they keep using it. The dynamic matters for enterprise leaders and workforce planners because Gen Z is now the fastest-entering cohort in professional environments, and their relationship with AI tools will shape adoption patterns, productivity norms, and organizational trust in the years ahead.

## Key Data Points

- Gallup surveyed **nearly 1,600 people ages 14–29** across the United States — one of the largest systematic studies of Gen Z's AI relationship to date
- Finding: young adults have grown **less hopeful and more angry** about artificial intelligence over the past year
- Usage rates remain high — approximately **half of Gen Z uses AI** — driven by its embedding in school assignments, workplace tools, and daily apps
- The study identifies a "hype wearing off" dynamic: AI has become infrastructure rather than novelty, eroding the excitement that earlier adoption drove
- Context: Gen Z entered the workforce as AI coding, writing, and analysis tools became standard — the group most expected to embrace AI-first workflows is the group reporting the strongest backlash
- The Verge notes the pattern: Gen Z is "increasingly disillusioned with AI — just not enough to stop using it"
- Parallel data point from AI Weekly Issue #481: tech layoffs passed **165,000** in early April 2026, with AI cited in **25% of March cuts** — up from single digits a year ago

## Why It Matters

For managers, HR leaders, and anyone building AI-first workflows, this finding is a direct signal: you cannot assume that younger employees are enthusiastic AI adopters. The cohort most exposed to AI at school and early in their careers is processing genuine frustration — likely driven by AI's role in labor market displacement, academic integrity pressures, and the gap between AI's promises and its actual outputs. The personal-stake angle for professional audiences is concrete: if you are deploying AI tools for a team that includes Gen Z workers, the change management challenge is bigger than you may have planned for. Anger and distrust do not prevent use — but they do predict resistance, workarounds, and burnout in AI-mandated workflows.
