# Why Gen Z Is Booing the AI Evangelists (and Why They're Right To)

## The trigger event

Four AI evangelists were booed at commencement speeches this spring:
- **Eric Schmidt (former Google CEO)** — University of Arizona, sustained loud booing in front of ~10,000 graduates, loud enough to make the news
- **A real estate executive** — University of Central Florida, booed while saying "AI is the next industrial revolution"
- **CEO of Big Machine Records** — Middle Tennessee State
- **An Adobe AI evangelist** — Marquette University

Four speakers, four schools, same reception.

## The core thesis

The booing is the most rational response to commencement-stage AI talk. It's not really about AI. AI is the most recent thing being handed to a generation that has been on the losing end of a much longer transfer.

## Key data points (all sourced in article)

### The labor market for grads
- **Unemployment for college grads aged 22-27 is at its highest level in 12 years.**
- **~70% of college students see AI as a threat to their job prospects.**
- The entry-level white-collar pipeline (analyst seat, junior associate role, first-year coordinator) is the exact layer AI tooling is being pointed at.
- AI hasn't replaced the junior analyst role outright. **A senior person with good AI tools can now plausibly do the work of three juniors, and finance departments noticed.**

### The 40-year wealth transfer
- Households headed by people **over 70** used to control **~19%** of national wealth.
- Households **under 40** used to control **~12%**.
- The over-70 share has climbed; **the under-40 share has been roughly cut in half**.
- A 30-year-old today is, **for the first time in modern US history, doing worse than their parents were at 30**: less buying power, less home equity, more debt, later family formation.

### How the transfer happened
- **Minimum wage hasn't kept pace with productivity** — if it had, it would be well over **$20/hour**.
- **Home prices have decoupled from household income** — largely because existing owners use zoning, permitting, and local politics to block new supply.
- Higher education ran the same playbook: restrict supply, raise price, harvest aspiration. Schools admit at single-digit acceptance rates while charging multiples of what a generation ago paid.
- **COVID accelerated it**: asset prices were rescued; wage earners got a few checks; people who already owned things ended the pandemic dramatically richer.

### Mental health and family formation
- Self-harm rates among young people, especially young women, are at historic highs.
- Depression, loneliness, and "deaths of despair" among the young have all moved in the wrong direction.
- The share of people in their early thirties who have had a child has **roughly halved in a generation**.

## The apprenticeship argument (the part that should make everyone uncomfortable)

The author worked ~20 years. What makes him effective with AI is 20 years of pattern-matching across many domains. When he looks at an output, he can tell whether it's good. He knows what happens to it after he hands it off, what will break in three weeks, what the question behind the question is. **That judgment is what gets multiplied.**

A 22-year-old doesn't have that yet. They were going to get it the way everyone before them got it: doing the messy grunt work for three to five years. Reading the bad contracts. Building the ugly first model. Sitting in meetings where they didn't understand half of what was said and had to figure out which half mattered.

**The AI tools are eating that exact layer.** The work still has to happen; companies aren't eliminating it. They're eliminating the person who used to do it as training. The output gets produced faster and cheaper, and the apprenticeship that used to come bundled with the output gets thrown out.

**The question that should make every founder uncomfortable:** Where do the senior people with good judgment come from in 10 years if nobody is doing the apprenticeship now?

"Learn to use AI" is not an answer. Prompting an LLM does not, by itself, teach you what a good output looks like. You have to have seen a lot of outputs in context, with consequences attached, to know.

**Training that only teaches people to produce outputs faster accelerates the collapse. Training that teaches them to evaluate, revise, and contest what an AI produces is what rebuilds the judgment layer.**

## Where the graduates are right

They're seeing something the people running the ceremonies haven't had to see yet. The criticism that some used AI to cheat coursework and are now complaining about AI eating their jobs is fair — but both things can be true. The students who used AI to skip the learning are walking into a market that is using AI to skip hiring them. They got hit from both directions.

## The author's actual advice (instead of "embrace the future")

- The next five years are going to be unusually hard for this cohort specifically.
- The apprenticeship pipeline is being restructured while they walk into it.
- The economic deck has been stacked for longer than they've been alive — AI is the newest card, not the first one.
- The most valuable thing you can do is **find the messy, unglamorous, high-context work that AI is bad at, and plant yourself there for a while. Get the reps. Build the judgment. Be the person who can tell whether the output is right. That role is not going away. The role that just produces the output is.**

## The harder ask (the part the author "almost never hears")

The older generation owes Gen Z something — not just sympathy. **A redesign**: of zoning, of school funding, of how we tax capital vs labor, of how we decide who is eligible for the safety net, of how we let companies offload the cost of training onto a public that increasingly can't afford to absorb it. The transfer wasn't natural law. It was a series of choices. Different choices are available.

## The closing point

What the booing rejects isn't the technology. It's **the act of being congratulated for graduating into a market that is restructuring the first ten years of their career before they enter it while a stranger on stage calls it progress.**

The apprenticeship problem is not just theirs. It's going to be everyone's, one cohort at a time, until somebody figures out where the next generation of judgment comes from.

## Why this matters for Grey AI's audience

This is a **founder / team-lead decision-changing piece**. The thesis directly affects how a founder thinks about:
- Hiring (do you skip the junior hire because AI does the work cheaper?)
- Training (how do you build judgment in your team if the apprenticeship layer is gone?)
- Org design (where does the next generation of senior operators come from?)
- Talent strategy (the contrarian play is to hire juniors and train them on AI-evaluation, not AI-production)

The "X isn't Y, it's Z" reframe is built in: **"Gen Z booing AI isn't about AI — it's about being congratulated for entering a market that just sawed off the bottom rung."**

Or for founders: **"AI isn't replacing the junior analyst — it's eliminating the apprenticeship that used to come bundled with the work, and that's a 10-year senior-talent problem dressed up as a 1-year cost saving."**
