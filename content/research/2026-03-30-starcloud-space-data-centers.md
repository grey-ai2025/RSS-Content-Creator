# Starcloud Raises $170M to Build Data Centers in Orbit — Fastest YC Startup to Hit Unicorn Status

**Publication Date:** March 30, 2026

**Keyword Match:** Physical AI and robotics (#9) — exceptional exception: $170M raise + landmark infrastructure development qualifying under the 2-story newsworthy exception; also relevant to AI infrastructure spending

## Summary

Starcloud, a startup building data centers in Earth's orbit, has raised $170 million in a Series A round led by Benchmark and EQT Ventures, valuing the company at $1.1 billion. The funding makes Starcloud the fastest Y Combinator startup to reach unicorn status — just 17 months after its demo day. The company has already launched its first satellite carrying an Nvidia H100 GPU, used it to train an AI model in orbit (a claimed first), and run a version of Google Gemini from space. Its next satellite, Starcloud 2, will carry Nvidia Blackwell chips and an AWS server blade. The long-term vision is a data center spacecraft launched from SpaceX's Starship, designed to deliver compute costs competitive with terrestrial data centers at roughly $0.05 per kWh — but that depends on Starship achieving commercial launch cadence, which CEO Philip Johnston expects around 2028–2029. Competitors include Google's Project Suncatcher, Aetherflux (reportedly raising at a $2B valuation), Aethero, and potentially SpaceX itself, which has sought permission to build a million-satellite distributed compute network.

## Why It Matters

Space-based computing is no longer science fiction pitch material — it is now attracting benchmark-led Series A rounds and unicorn valuations. The core driver is practical: Earth-based data center construction is being slowed by resource constraints, regulatory resistance, and power grid limitations. Investors are placing early bets on the possibility that orbital infrastructure could eventually offload inference workloads that do not require ultra-low latency. For enterprise and infrastructure leaders tracking AI's physical footprint, this story illustrates how far the investment thesis around AI infrastructure has expanded — from GPU chips to submarine cables to satellites. The near-term economics remain unproven and Starship-dependent, but the scale of capital flowing into this space signals that serious institutional investors believe orbital compute is a viable long-term play.
