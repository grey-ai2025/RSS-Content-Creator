## Summary
Companies that pay security researchers to find software vulnerabilities — bug bounty programs — are being overwhelmed by AI-generated false reports, forcing some to suspend their programs entirely. Bugcrowd, whose clients include OpenAI, T-Mobile, and Motorola, saw its report volume quadruple over a three-week period in March, with most submissions proving to be false. Security software company Curl suspended its paid program in January after an "explosion in AI slop reports." Nextcloud suspended its program in April for the same reason. The problem is driven by a three-tier wave: amateurs using AI to try bug hunting for the first time, experienced researchers being misled by AI tools, and a third group of "experienced AI builders" who have built fully automated end-to-end scanning and submission pipelines. The same week this news broke, Anthropic launched its Mythos cybersecurity model — which is designed to find software flaws faster than humans, likely to accelerate this dynamic further.

## Key Data Points
- **Bugcrowd**: report volume more than quadrupled over a three-week period in March 2026; most proved to be false
- **Curl** (widely-used internet data transfer tool): suspended paid bug bounty program in January 2026; creator Daniel Stenberg said the "never-ending slop" took "a serious mental toll to manage and sometimes also a long time to debunk"
- **Nextcloud**: suspended bug bounty program in April 2026 due to "massive increase of low-quality reports"
- **HackerOne** (serves Goldman Sachs, Google, US Department of Defense): submissions jumped **76 percent** year-over-year to March 2026; share of legitimate vulnerability reports held steady at **25 percent** — meaning the other 75% are now noise
- HackerOne CEO Kara Sprague: rise in "higher quality" reports using AI also observed, suggesting a bifurcation — skilled researchers using AI well, while a larger volume of low-skill AI noise drowns the signal
- Anthropic launched **Mythos** last month — a cyber AI model designed to find software flaws faster than humans; Bugcrowd CEO Dave Gerry: "AI is going to help with a lot of things but we're never going to replace that human creativity"
- Sophos CISO Ross McKerchar: the surge is "quickly becoming a major problem" and "bug bounties are going to stay [but] they're going to have to change"
- Three identified cohorts driving the flood: (1) first-time amateurs using AI, (2) experienced researchers being misled by AI tools, (3) "experienced AI builders" running "end-to-end scanning and submission systems"
- Response: companies introducing stricter background checks and building AI triage agents to filter AI-generated submissions — an AI-vs-AI arms race

## Why It Matters
For any SMB or startup running a bug bounty program, vendor due-diligence process, or security review workflow, this story documents a direct operational cost from the AI content flood. The 76% surge in raw submissions with a flat 25% legitimate hit rate means security teams are now doing three times more triage work for the same yield. But the broader pattern is the more important signal for founders and operators: any process that accepts external input — security reports, job applications, RFP responses, customer feedback — is now subject to this same AI-flood dynamic. The two-track internet framing from this week's SEO keywords applies here: one track is human-generated signal, one track is AI-generated noise, and distinguishing them is becoming a new operational cost center. For SMB operators, the practical question is: which of your intake processes have suddenly become high-volume and low-signal, and do you have the triage infrastructure to handle that?
