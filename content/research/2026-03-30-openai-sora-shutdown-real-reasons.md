# OpenAI Shut Down Sora Because It Was a $1M-a-Day Money Pit Nobody Was Using

**Source URL:** https://techcrunch.com/2026/03/29/why-openai-really-shut-down-sora/

**Publication Date:** March 30, 2026

**Keyword Match:** Generative AI in enterprise (#7); AI reliability and trust (#5)

## Summary

OpenAI's decision to shut down Sora — its AI video-generation app launched just six months earlier — was not a data grab or a strategic pivot. According to a Wall Street Journal investigation, Sora was a financial catastrophe: user numbers peaked at around one million and then collapsed to fewer than 500,000, while the app burned through approximately $1 million every day in compute costs. CEO Sam Altman made the call to kill Sora, free up chips, and refocus the company's resources on products that actually drive revenue. The most glaring competitive context: while OpenAI kept a dedicated team on Sora, Anthropic was quietly winning over the software engineers and enterprise customers that matter most. Claude Code, in particular, was cutting into OpenAI's core business. Disney, which had committed $1 billion to a partnership with OpenAI built around Sora, found out the product was being discontinued less than an hour before the public announcement — and the deal died with it.

## Key Data Points

- Sora's global user count peaked at approximately 1 million, then fell to fewer than 500,000
- The app was costing OpenAI roughly $1 million per day to operate
- Disney had committed $1 billion to a partnership built around Sora and learned of the shutdown less than one hour before the public announcement
- OpenAI is now redirecting compute resources freed from Sora toward business and productivity use cases
- Claude Code was identified as a direct competitive threat eating into OpenAI's developer and enterprise revenue
- OpenAI's own team was "refocusing" on agentic and productivity tools rather than consumer video generation

## Why It Matters

For enterprise and product leaders, Sora's collapse is a rare, unusually transparent case study in what happens when AI product strategy drifts from where customers actually pay. The company that arguably started the generative AI wave could not sustain a consumer video product against better-focused competitors. The $1M/day burn rate with sub-500K active users is a brutal unit economics lesson. It also signals a broader shift in where the AI value chain is hardening: not consumer-facing media generation, but developer tooling, agentic workflows, and enterprise productivity. The fallout for Disney also raises a governance question that will resonate with enterprise buyers: when a major AI vendor abruptly kills a product you've bet a billion dollars on, what does your vendor risk management strategy look like?
