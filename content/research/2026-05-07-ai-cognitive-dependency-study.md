## Summary
A new study by researchers at Carnegie Mellon, MIT, Oxford, and UCLA found that brief AI chatbot use — as little as 10 minutes — significantly reduces people's ability to persist at and solve problems when the AI is taken away. Across three experiments involving several hundred people each, participants given AI assistance became measurably more likely to give up on tasks or produce wrong answers once the AI helper was removed. The researcher's framing is careful: the takeaway is not to ban AI, but to recognize that AI tools optimized for immediate productivity can trade away the development of foundational problem-solving skills. The study's lead researcher at MIT argues that AI design may need to shift toward coaching and scaffolding rather than answer-giving. The finding is particularly sharp because the willingness to persist through difficulty is the primary predictor of long-term skill acquisition — meaning AI reliance today may be compounding into a workforce capability gap tomorrow.

## Key Data Points
- Three experiments, each involving several hundred participants, conducted through an online paid task platform
- Tasks included simple fractions and reading comprehension — foundational cognitive skills
- When AI was removed mid-task, AI-assisted participants were "significantly more likely to give up or flub their answers"
- Lead researcher MIT Assist. Prof. Michiel Bakker: "We should be more careful about what kind of help AI provides, and when"
- Study inspired by a well-known essay on "gradual disempowerment" via AI dependency
- Bakker: "Systems that give direct answers may have very different long-term effects from systems that scaffold, coach, or challenge the user"
- Agentic AI systems flagged as "particularly unpredictable" because they do complex chores independently and can introduce odd errors
- OpenAI has already been attempting to reduce sycophancy in newer GPT releases as a related concern
- Author's personal anecdote: AI assistant Linux commands bricked his machine, illustrating cost of offloading critical thinking
- Institutions involved: Carnegie Mellon, MIT, Oxford, UCLA

## Why It Matters
For a founder or team lead deciding how to integrate AI tools across a 10-50 person company, this study reframes the ROI calculation. Short-term productivity gains from AI may come with a hidden cost: the gradual erosion of the judgment and persistence that makes a team capable of solving novel problems. This matters most for early-career employees who are still building foundational skills, and for roles where problem-solving is the product (product, engineering, strategy, operations). The practical implication is not to remove AI tools, but to think about how they are deployed: answer-delivery vs. coaching-style tools will have very different long-term effects on team capability. Teams running AI-first workflows may be winning quarters while losing the war on judgment development.
