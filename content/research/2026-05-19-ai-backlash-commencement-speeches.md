## Summary
Two high-profile commencement speakers who praised AI were booed by graduates in back-to-back incidents this week. Former Google CEO Eric Schmidt was drowned out by boos at the University of Arizona after telling students they should "get on the rocketship" of AI — before he could even finish his point about AI agents assembling teams. At the University of Central Florida, real estate executive Gloria Caulfield triggered successive rounds of booing the moment she declared "the rise of artificial intelligence is the next industrial revolution." Both incidents reveal that young professionals entering the workforce are not just skeptical of AI hype — they are actively hostile to it. The pattern is not spontaneous anger; it reflects a documented collapse in job-market confidence among the demographic most directly in the path of AI displacement.

## Key Data Points
- Former Google CEO Eric Schmidt booed at University of Arizona commencement, May 16, 2026; boos began even before he took the stage, partly due to ongoing sexual assault lawsuit allegations
- Schmidt's pro-AI line — "You can now assemble a team of AI agents to help you with the parts you could never accomplish on your own" — was shouted down
- Gloria Caulfield booed at UCF the prior week; the crowd cheered loudly moments later when she inadvertently acknowledged "Only a few years ago, AI was not a factor in our lives"
- **Gallup poll**: only 43% of Americans aged 15 to 34 said it's a good time to find a job locally — down from 75% in 2022
- Schmidt himself acknowledged the sentiment: "There is a fear in your generation that the future has already been written, that the machines are coming, that the jobs are evaporating"
- Journalist Brian Merchant framed AI as "the cruel new face of hyper-scaling capitalism" for this generation
- Jensen Huang at Carnegie Mellon's commencement received no audible pushback — contrast attributed to audience (engineering graduates vs. arts/humanities)
- NYT commencement roundup framed "resilience" as this year's dominant theme — a defensive frame rather than an optimistic one
- Student Alexander Rose Tyson on the UCF booing: "It wasn't one person that really started the booing. It was just sort of like a collective, 'This sucks.'"

## Why It Matters
For founders, SMB operators, and team leads, this is a concrete signal about the workforce they are hiring into and managing. The generation entering the job market right now — the people who will staff their companies, train on their tools, and sell to their customers — has gone from cautiously optimistic to openly hostile about AI. That is not a communication problem; it is a strategic one. Leaders who frame AI adoption internally as purely a productivity play, without addressing what it means for the careers of the people using it, are going to face exactly this kind of resistance — just inside the building instead of on a graduation stage. The Gallup data (job confidence down from 75% to 43% in three years) tells the structural story: AI deployment has already reshaped what young workers believe is waiting for them. The practical ask: any AI rollout that doesn't have an honest narrative about what it means for the people doing the work will breed resentment, not adoption.
