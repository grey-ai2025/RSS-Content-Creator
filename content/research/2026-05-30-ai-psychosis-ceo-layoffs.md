# "AI Psychosis": What Happens When CEOs Are Too Far From the Work to Know What AI Can't Do

**Source URL:** https://techcrunch.com/2026/05/27/tech-ceos-are-apparently-suffering-from-ai-psychosis/

**Publication Date:** May 27, 2026 (within 48h window as of May 30)

**Keyword Match:** AI and the future of work (Trending Topic #9); AI agents / agentic AI (Trending Topic #1)

---

## Summary

Box CEO Aaron Levie coined the phrase "AI psychosis" to describe a growing pattern: tech executives who are sufficiently removed from day-to-day operations test AI prototypes, see promising results, and then dramatically overestimate what full automation can deliver — leading to workforce cuts that damage their businesses. ClickUp is the most cited example: CEO Zeb Evans laid off 22% of the company's workforce (roughly 250 people) after deploying approximately 3,000 internal AI agents, claiming the move would create a "100x organization." In the first five months of 2026, 115,430 employees have been cut from 152 tech companies, with many layoff announcements explicitly citing AI productivity gains as justification. Levie's counter-argument: CEOs who haven't personally used AI extensively — and understand both its capabilities and its very real limitations — are making these decisions blind.

---

## Key Data Points

- Term coined by **Aaron Levie** (Box CEO): "AI psychosis" — executives disconnected from operational realities making AI-driven staffing decisions
- **ClickUp** laid off **22% of its workforce** (~250 people) to make room for approximately **3,000 internal AI agents**; CEO called it building a "100x organization"
- **115,430 employees** cut from **152 tech companies** in the first five months of 2026 — many explicitly citing AI
- Levie: "CEOs are uniquely prone to AI psychosis because they're sufficiently distant from the last mile of work that still has to happen"
- 2026 tech layoffs are already nearly matching the full-year total for 2025
- Levie's prescription: executives should personally use AI extensively to build accurate mental models of what it can and cannot do
- Cognition CEO Scott Wu (maker of AI coding agent Devin): "We've never thought about it as replacing humans… it should always be up to the human what to do"
- Microsoft reported that AI agents can cost **more per task** than human employees in some workflows — underscoring the "real cost problem" (keyword phrase)
- Pattern: leaders demo AI prototypes in controlled conditions, extrapolate to full operational replacement, miss the "last mile" complexity

---

## Why It Matters

This story is a direct mirror for founders and team leads making AI adoption decisions. The risk isn't just for enterprise CEOs — any SMB operator who has watched an AI demo and thought "I could replace my customer service team" is susceptible to the same trap. The practical test Levie describes — using AI yourself, extensively, before deciding what it replaces — is actionable for any business owner. The ClickUp example is also a cautionary tale about the gap between agent deployment (easy to count) and actual output quality (hard to measure until something breaks). For anyone evaluating AI agents for their team right now, the question isn't "can this agent do the task?" but "what is the last mile of human judgment this agent will miss, and what does it cost when it misses it?" The 2026 layoff data also creates real reputational and morale risk for companies that frame cuts as AI-driven — even if the underlying economics are sound.
